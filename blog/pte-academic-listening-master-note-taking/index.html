PTE Listening Note-Taking: A Practical Guide for 2026

By Aakarsh 14 October 2024 7 min read

In PTE Academic you hear every recording only once. There is no replay button, and several of the highest-value tasks ask you to summarise or retell what you heard. That is why PTE listening note-taking is one of the most useful skills you can build. Good notes are not a transcript; they are a quick map of the main idea, the key supporting points and the conclusion, which you can turn into a written or spoken answer a few seconds later. This guide explains which tasks need notes, a simple system for taking them, and worked examples for the main task types, including the newer speaking tasks added in 2025.

Which PTE tasks need notes?

Pearson’s official guidance says you are allowed to take notes during the Listening part, and you are given an erasable noteboard booklet and pen at the test centre. Notes are not equally useful for every task, so it helps to know where to focus.

Task Section Audio length How notes help
Summarize Spoken Text Listening 60 to 90 seconds Essential: main point plus supporting points for a 50 to 70 word summary
Retell Lecture Speaking and Writing Up to 90 seconds Essential: you speak for 40 seconds after 10 seconds’ preparation
Summarize Group Discussion Speaking and Writing Up to 3 minutes, three speakers Essential: you speak for up to 2 minutes after 10 seconds’ preparation
Multiple Choice, Multiple Answers Listening 80 to 120 seconds Very useful: details may not come in the same order as the options
Fill in the Blanks Listening 30 to 60 seconds Useful: jot down the missing words, then check spelling
Write from Dictation Listening 3 to 5 seconds Useful: quick first letters or short words to hold the sentence
Respond to a Situation Speaking and Writing Prompt up to 60 words (listen and read) Light notes: who, what problem, what you need to say

For Repeat Sentence and Answer Short Question, there is no time to write, so rely on memory and focus on listening for meaningful chunks.

A simple PTE listening note-taking system

1. Prepare the page

Before each long recording starts, draw a quick structure on your noteboard. For lectures, three lines are enough: Topic, Points, Conclusion. For Summarize Group Discussion, draw three narrow columns, one for each speaker, because you need to report who said what.

2. Write keywords, not sentences

Write nouns, numbers, names and verbs that carry the meaning. Leave out articles, auxiliary verbs and filler words. You will rebuild the grammar when you write or speak.

3. Use abbreviations and symbols

Develop a personal set and use it in every practice session, so it becomes automatic. Here is a starter set:

Symbol or abbreviation Meaning
↑ / ↓ increase, rise / decrease, fall
→ leads to, causes, results in
= is, means, equals
≠ is not, differs from
+ / vs and, also / compared with, versus
b/c because
e.g. for example
govt, edu, env, tech, econ government, education, environment, technology, economy
imp, prob, sol important, problem, solution
%, K, M per cent, thousand, million

4. Listen for signpost language

Speakers tell you what is coming. Phrases such as “the main reason is”, “however”, “on the other hand”, “for example”, “as a result” and “to sum up” signal a new point, a contrast, an example or the conclusion. When you hear “however”, draw a line or write “BUT”, because a contrast is often a key point in the summary.

5. Capture the conclusion

Many students stop writing in the last 15 seconds because they feel they already have enough. The conclusion or final recommendation is often what turns an average summary into a complete one, so keep writing until the audio stops.

Worked examples by task type

Summarize Spoken Text

You hear a 60 to 90 second recording, then have 10 minutes to write a summary of 50 to 70 words. Pearson’s official tips say to note the supporting points as well as the main idea. Content, form, grammar, vocabulary and spelling are all scored, and a summary that misreads the topic scores zero, as does one shorter than 40 words or longer than 100.

Sample notes (lecture on urban trees):

Topic: urban trees → city health
1. shade ↓ temp (heat islands)
2. absorb pollut → air qual ↑
3. mental health ↑, stress ↓
BUT maint cost, need planning
Concl: cities shd invest + plan long-term

Sample summary (61 words):

The speaker argues that urban trees bring significant benefits to city life. Firstly, they provide shade, which lowers temperatures and reduces the heat island effect. Secondly, trees absorb pollutants and therefore improve air quality. They also support mental health by reducing stress. However, trees require maintenance and careful planning, so the speaker concludes that cities should invest in long-term planting strategies.

Leave one or two minutes to check grammar, spelling and the word count, and use one spelling convention (British or American) consistently.

Retell Lecture

You hear a lecture of up to 90 seconds, sometimes with an image, then get 10 seconds to prepare and 40 seconds to speak. Use the same Topic, Points, Conclusion notes and turn them into a simple spoken frame:

“The lecture was about… The speaker first explained that… He or she also mentioned… In addition… Finally, the speaker concluded that…”

Do not try to read every word you wrote. Pick the main idea, two or three key points and the conclusion, and keep speaking smoothly. Fluency and pronunciation are scored alongside content.

Summarize Group Discussion

Added in 2025, this task plays a discussion of up to three minutes between three people. You then have 10 seconds to prepare and two minutes to speak, summarising the discussion in your own words. Column notes work best:

Topic: remote work for new staff
A: flexible, saves commute, prod ↑
B: new staff miss mentoring, feel isolated
C: hybrid = compromise, 2-3 days office
Agreed: hybrid trial

In your answer, state the topic, give each speaker’s view, then the outcome or point of agreement. Pearson advises using the full two minutes to cover all main points without repeating ideas or stopping to correct yourself.

Multiple Choice, Multiple Answers

Use the seven seconds before the recording starts to read the question. Then take notes rather than clicking options while you listen, because the information may not come in the same order as the options. Choose answers that match the meaning of what you heard, not just the same words. Partial credit scoring applies and incorrect choices count against you, so only select options you are confident about.

Fill in the Blanks and Write from Dictation

For Fill in the Blanks, skim the transcript before the audio starts, then jot each missing word on your noteboard as you hear it and type it carefully afterwards. For Write from Dictation, write the first few letters of each word in a row (for example, “stu mst sub ass by Fri” for “Students must submit their assignments by Friday”), then type the full sentence immediately while it is fresh.

Common note-taking mistakes

  • Writing too much: if you try to write full sentences, you will miss the next point.
  • Missing key details: numbers, names and contrasts are often what the answer depends on.
  • Unreadable notes: leave space between points and keep symbols consistent so you can read them back in seconds.
  • Copying the speaker word for word: in summary tasks you must use your own words; unoriginal responses receive no credit.
  • Practising without timing: always practise with the real time limits so your speed improves.

Practice routine that builds speed

  1. Listen to a short podcast or lecture clip of about 90 seconds once only, taking notes.
  2. Write a 50 to 70 word summary in under 10 minutes.
  3. Using the same notes, retell it aloud in 40 seconds and record yourself.
  4. Replay the clip and check what you missed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take notes in the PTE Academic test?

Yes. You receive an erasable noteboard booklet and pen at the test centre, and Pearson’s guidance encourages note-taking for tasks such as Summarize Spoken Text, Retell Lecture and Summarize Group Discussion.

How many words should I write for Summarize Spoken Text?

Between 50 and 70 words. Writing outside this range lowers your form score, and a response under 40 words or over 100 words receives no score for the task.

Do I need notes for Repeat Sentence?

Generally no. The sentence is only 3 to 9 seconds long and you must begin speaking straight away, so it is better to listen for meaningful phrases and repeat them with the same stress and intonation.
