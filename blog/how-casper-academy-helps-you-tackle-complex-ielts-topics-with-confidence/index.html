IELTS Writing Task 2 Difficult Topics: How to Handle Them

By Aakarsh 9 October 2024 7 min read

You open the Writing paper, read the Task 2 question and your mind goes blank. The topic is space exploration, genetic engineering or the role of museums, and you have never thought about it in your life. This happens to strong candidates too. The good news is that IELTS Writing Task 2 difficult topics are not testing your general knowledge. They test whether you can take an unfamiliar issue, form a clear position and support it in organised English. This guide shows you a repeatable method for handling those questions, with a worked example, and explains how the same approach works in Speaking Part 3.

Why some Task 2 topics feel so hard

In Task 2 you are given a point of view, argument or problem to discuss, and you must write at least 250 words. The official guidance suggests spending about 40 minutes on it, and Task 2 counts for twice as much as Task 1 in your Writing score. So a panic on a difficult topic can cost you a lot.

Topics usually feel difficult for one of three reasons:

  • Abstract ideas: questions about values, ethics, tradition or progress, with nothing concrete to hold on to.
  • Unfamiliar fields: science funding, urban planning, international aid or the arts.
  • Complex wording: two-part questions, or prompts that ask about one narrow aspect of a wide topic.

Remember what the examiner actually marks: Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. None of these criteria rewards expert knowledge. Simple, relevant ideas that are well developed will score better than clever ideas that are badly explained.

A five-step method for IELTS Writing Task 2 difficult topics

Step 1: Decode the question (2 minutes)

Underline the topic, the specific focus and the instruction. Ask: what exactly am I being asked to do? Is it agree or disagree, discuss both views, advantages and disadvantages, problem and solution, or a two-part question? The official IELTS guidance warns that if the question is about one aspect of a topic, you must write about that aspect only. Writing about “technology in general” when the question is about “technology in primary schools” is one of the fastest ways to lose Task Response marks.

Step 2: Generate ideas using familiar angles (3 minutes)

When you know nothing about a topic, run it through a quick checklist of angles that apply to almost anything:

  • People: individuals, families, children, the elderly
  • Money: cost, jobs, economy, taxes
  • Health and safety
  • Education and skills
  • Environment
  • Technology
  • Society and culture: tradition, identity, equality

You only need two strong ideas for a well-developed essay. Pick the two angles where you can explain a clear cause and effect.

Step 3: Decide your position and plan (3 to 5 minutes)

Choose a position you can defend easily, even if it is not your deepest personal belief. Then plan four paragraphs: introduction, two body paragraphs and a conclusion. Each body paragraph needs one main idea, an explanation and an example.

Step 4: Use specific, believable examples

Examples do not need to be statistics. A realistic example from your own experience or general knowledge is acceptable, and the official guidance says examples can come from your own experience. For instance, the way UPI payments spread across small shops in Kerala is a perfectly good example for an essay about technology and the economy. Avoid inventing precise figures, because made-up numbers often sound unconvincing.

Step 5: Write, then check (remaining time)

Write clearly and keep 3 to 4 minutes at the end to check for grammar slips, missing articles and repeated words. If you need help with this final check, see our list of grammar rules for IELTS writing.

Worked example: a difficult Task 2 question

Some people believe that governments should spend money on space exploration, while others think this money should be used to solve problems on Earth. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Decode: discuss both views, then give a clear opinion. Focus: government spending, not private companies.

Ideas using familiar angles:

  • For space spending (technology, economy): satellite technology supports weather forecasting, communication and disaster warnings; it creates skilled jobs.
  • For Earth problems (people, health): poverty, healthcare and clean water need urgent funding; benefits are immediate and visible.
  • Opinion: a balanced approach, with most funds for urgent needs and a smaller, focused space budget that brings practical benefits.

Sample introduction:

Governments have limited budgets, so deciding whether to fund space research or urgent problems at home is a difficult choice. While some argue that exploring space is a luxury, others see it as an investment in the future. In my view, governments should prioritise pressing needs on Earth but continue to fund space programmes that deliver practical benefits.

Sample body paragraph:

Supporters of space exploration point out that it often produces technology that improves daily life on Earth. Satellites, for example, allow meteorologists to track cyclones days in advance, which gives coastal communities time to evacuate. In a country with a long coastline such as India, this kind of early warning can save many lives. Seen this way, part of the money spent on space research is also money spent on solving problems at home.

Notice the pattern: a topic sentence, an explanation, a concrete example and a link back to the question. You can use this structure for any topic, however unfamiliar.

Handling complex topics in Speaking Part 3

Speaking Part 3 is a discussion of 4 to 5 minutes in which the examiner asks more general and abstract questions linked to your Part 2 topic. It tests your ability to explain opinions and to analyse, discuss and speculate about issues. The same thinking skills from Task 2 apply, just faster.

Use a simple answer structure

  1. Answer directly: give your view in the first sentence.
  2. Give a reason: explain why.
  3. Add an example or contrast: a real situation or the other side.
  4. Speculate if relevant: how this might change in future.

Sample Part 3 answer

Examiner: Do you think museums are still important now that people can find information online?

I think they are, although their role has changed. Online, you can read about almost anything, but you cannot really experience it. When I visited a heritage museum as a student, seeing the old tools and boats made the history of the region feel real in a way a website never did. Having said that, museums will probably need to use more technology, such as virtual tours, if they want to attract younger visitors.

Useful language for unfamiliar questions

  • Buying a moment to think: “That is an interesting question. I have not thought about it much, but I would say…”
  • Hedging: “It depends to some extent on…”, “I suppose one reason might be…”
  • Contrasting: “On the other hand…”, “Having said that…”
  • Speculating: “In the future, it is likely that…”, “I would expect…”

It is perfectly acceptable to say you do not know much about a topic, as long as you then give a reasoned opinion. For more on the format, read IELTS Speaking Test: Everything You Need to Know.

Common pitfalls to avoid

  • Going off-topic: writing about the general area instead of the specific question.
  • Overgeneralising: claims like “everyone agrees” or “this always happens” weaken your argument. Use “many people”, “often” or “in most cases”.
  • Lack of clarity: long, tangled sentences that try to sound academic. Clear and accurate beats complicated and confusing.
  • Memorised paragraphs: examiners recognise them, and they rarely fit the exact question.
  • No clear position: if the question asks for your opinion, state it in the introduction and keep it consistent.
  • Repeating the same words: build topic vocabulary in advance. Our post on vocabulary for a higher IELTS Writing band can help.

Frequently asked questions

What if I know nothing about the Task 2 topic?

Use the familiar angles checklist (people, money, health, education, environment, technology, society) to find two ideas you can explain. The examiner marks your language and how well you respond to the question, not your expert knowledge.

Can I use personal examples in IELTS Writing Task 2?

Yes. The official IELTS guidance says examples can come from your own experience. Keep them brief, relevant and clearly linked to your main point.

How many ideas should a Task 2 essay have?

Two well-developed main ideas, one per body paragraph, are usually enough. Developing each idea with an explanation and an example is more valuable than listing many points.
