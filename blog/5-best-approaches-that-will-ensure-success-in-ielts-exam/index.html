How to Crack IELTS in the First Attempt: 7 Proven Steps

By casperblog_admin 14 June 2022 6 min read

Almost every IELTS aspirant asks the same two questions: how should I prepare, and how to crack IELTS in the first attempt? A retake costs money and time, and it can delay a university intake or a visa application. The good news is that most first-attempt failures come from a handful of avoidable mistakes. This guide brings together the approaches that work, from making English part of your daily life to treating the Writing test with the seriousness it deserves.

Step 1: Know the exam inside out

Success starts with understanding exactly what you will face: the modules, the timings, the rules and how you are scored. Many candidates lose marks simply because they are surprised by a question type or run out of time.

Module Time Key facts
Listening About 30 minutes 4 parts, 40 questions, each recording played once
Reading 60 minutes 3 sections, 40 questions
Writing 60 minutes Task 1 (at least 150 words, about 20 minutes) and Task 2 (at least 250 words, about 40 minutes)
Speaking 11 to 14 minutes 3 parts, face to face with an examiner

You receive a band from 1 to 9 for each module, and your overall band is the average of the four, rounded to the nearest half or whole band. This means a weak score in one module pulls everything down, even if the other three are strong. You can try different combinations with our IELTS band score calculator.

Also check whether you need IELTS Academic (usually for university study) or IELTS General Training (often for work and migration), and confirm the exact band your university or visa requires before you book a date. Our checklist of things to do before booking your IELTS exam date will help.

Step 2: Switch to English everywhere

The single best long-term habit is to make English part of your everyday life, not just something you study for an hour a day. For non-native speakers, this steady exposure builds the listening, vocabulary and fluency that no shortcut can replace.

  • Watch and listen in English: switch to English news, shows and films. Use English subtitles at first if you find it difficult, then try watching without them.
  • Talk in English at home: if possible, hold some of your daily conversations with friends or family in English. You will help each other improve speaking and listening.
  • Read regularly: books, newspapers and good magazine articles show you a wide variety of sentence structures and grammar in use.
  • Play with words: crosswords, scrambled words and similar games in your free time are an enjoyable way to grow your vocabulary.

Step 3: Use authentic materials and avoid time-wasters

Many aspirants go wrong by blindly following random tips, videos, “secret tricks” and free materials shared online. Some of it is outdated, some of it does not match the real test, and some is simply wrong. What is free is not always good quality, and following poor material can build bad habits that are hard to break.

Instead, base your practice on authentic tests, such as the official Cambridge IELTS practice books, and on reliable official websites. Our guide to top IELTS books lists useful options.

Step 4: Practise with full mock tests

Even if you feel well prepared, do not take the test lightly. Timed practice tests teach you the pattern and difficulty level of each module, show you how your concentration holds up across the whole test, and reveal the mistakes you keep repeating.

The value of a mock test lies in what you do after it. For every wrong answer, ask why: did you misread the question, miss a keyword, exceed a word limit, or run out of time? Keep a simple error log and review it before each new practice test. Over a few weeks you will see the same mistakes disappear.

Step 5: Take the Writing test seriously

IELTS is known for its strict standards in Writing. Examiners assess task response, coherence and cohesion, vocabulary and grammatical range and accuracy. Many candidates find Listening and Reading relatively manageable, then lose their target overall band because of Writing.

What examiners look for in Task 2

  • A clear answer to the exact question asked, with a consistent position.
  • Logical paragraphs, each with one main idea that is explained and supported.
  • A range of accurate vocabulary, without forced or memorised phrases.
  • A mix of simple and complex sentences with few errors.

A worked example

Question: Some people believe that university education should be free for everyone. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

A weak introduction simply repeats the question and gives no position. A stronger one paraphrases the topic and states a clear view:

It is often argued that governments should pay the full cost of university education for all students. While I accept that free tuition would widen access, I largely disagree, because the cost would place a heavy burden on public finances and could reduce the quality of teaching.

A body paragraph should then develop one reason fully, for example by explaining how funding pressure might lead to larger class sizes, and supporting it with a specific example. Aim for at least 250 words, but focus on quality and clarity rather than length.

For more on building a strong word bank, read our post on getting a high IELTS Writing band with better vocabulary.

Step 6: Build quick wins in each module

Listening

Read the questions before each recording starts, predict the type of answer (a number, a name, a place) and watch spelling and word limits carefully.

Reading

Skim for the main idea, scan for keywords and their synonyms, and do not spend too long on one difficult question. For many question types, the answers follow the order of the text.

Speaking

Extend your answers with reasons and examples, speak at a natural pace, and do not memorise answers. In Part 2, use your one minute of preparation to note a few key points.

How to crack IELTS in the first attempt: a simple plan

  1. Take a diagnostic test and compare your result with your target band.
  2. Choose a test date that leaves enough time to close the gap, without rushing.
  3. Study every day, with extra time for your weakest module.
  4. Take a full mock test each week and review every mistake.
  5. Get regular feedback on Writing and Speaking.
  6. In the final week, focus on timing, rest and confidence rather than cramming.

Frequently asked questions

Is it realistic to crack IELTS in the first attempt?

Yes, for most well-prepared candidates. The key is to start from an honest assessment of your level, give yourself enough preparation time, practise with authentic material and get feedback on Writing and Speaking before test day.

How long should I prepare for IELTS?

It depends on the gap between your current level and your target band. Some students need a few weeks, others need a few months. Short preparation can work for confident English users, as explained in our post on whether you can prepare for IELTS in 10 days, but most people benefit from more time.

Which IELTS module is the hardest?

It varies from person to person, but many candidates find Writing the most difficult because it is assessed against strict criteria. That is why getting your essays corrected regularly is one of the most effective ways to improve your overall band.
